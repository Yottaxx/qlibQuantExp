# Notebook Archetypes — How Great Empiricists Actually Wrote

The four canonical reference points for the `notebook-the-anomaly` skill. Each is a practice that lasted decades and produced major science.

## 1. Faraday (1820–1862) — Diary + Idea Books

- 42 years of work, ~30,000 numbered experiments, all dated.
- Two strictly separated systems:
  - **Diary**: sequentially numbered experiments with date, conditions, results. Append-only.
  - **Idea Books** (and loose memoranda): raw speculation, "what if" conjectures, stray thoughts.
- Diary entries that graduated to publication were marked with **vertical lines in the margin** — a manual cross-reference system.
- Famous Faraday principle: *"Keep notes of all that occurs to you, however trifling it may at the time appear; not one observation in a thousand may be useful, but the loss of that one is what we cannot afford."*

**For this skill:** the Idea Book is what `notebook-the-anomaly` writes. The Diary is `qx-research-ledger`. The split is non-negotiable.

## 2. Darwin (1837–1838) — Notebooks B, C, D, E

- Within months of returning on the *Beagle*, Darwin filled notebooks B–E with the foundations of natural selection.
- **Speed**: Notebook D (1838) was filled in 3 months after reading Malthus.
- Style: short, dense, often a single sentence per idea. Many entries are questions, not conclusions.
- Cross-references are everywhere — Darwin would later return to an entry and append "see B p. 12" etc.

**For this skill:** when you're hot on a track, the notebook should be filled fast. Speed beats polish. Cross-reference shamelessly.

## 3. Edison (1870s onward) — the "10,000 ways that won't work"

- Edison's lab notebooks at Menlo Park ran to ~5 million pages.
- Distinctive feature: **negative results were logged with equal care to positive results**. The "10,000 ways that won't work" phrase (attributed to Edison) reflects this discipline: failures were data.
- Notebooks were considered legal documents (for patent disputes), so dates were precise.

**For this skill:** anomalies that *did not* pan out into an explanation still go in the notebook — they save future-you from trying the same thing again. Negative results are first-class entries.

## 4. Feynman (mid-20th century) — "Different Box" Mental Files

Feynman described keeping ~12 favorite open problems mentally, and whenever he heard a new technique or saw a new tool, he would test it against each of the 12. Occasionally a new tool unlocked one — and he'd be the first to solve it because he'd been ready.

**For this skill:** keep a list of unresolved anomalies. New surprises and new techniques should be checked against this list. If the new thing helps explain an old anomaly, that's a vertical-line moment — graduate the old entry.

## Common structure across the four

1. **Dated entries**, append-only, never edited.
2. **Raw over polished**. Speed and quantity over depth.
3. **Strict separation** of speculation and fact (Faraday explicit; others implicit via separate notebooks or sections).
4. **Cross-reference**: the value of an old entry depends on its retrievability.
5. **Negative results are logged**, not discarded.
